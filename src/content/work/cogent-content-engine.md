## 01 — The brief

Write and run marketing content for an IT services company across four
subject areas that all arrive pre-loaded with jargon: AI, cybersecurity,
upskilling and digital transformation. SEO articles, email, newsletters,
infographics and social — plus the campaigns that sat on top of them.

## 02 — The context

Enterprise B2B has a house style, and the house style is a problem. It is
formal, hedged, and written as though the reader is a procurement
committee rather than one person with a browser tab open. Every company
in the category writes it. Which means it is simultaneously the safest
choice and the reason nothing gets read.

The audience was not one audience either. Someone researching a
cybersecurity vendor and someone looking at upskilling options are in
completely different mental states, even if the brand talks to both.

## 03 — The observation

The pieces that performed were not the ones with the most authority in
the topic. They were the ones that got to a specific, usable point
fastest — and the ones that admitted a reader might not already care.

The clearest version of this was on the upskilling and careers side.
Content written in the register of a careers advisor did nothing. Content
written in the register of a person who had recently been on the job
market did considerably better. Same information. The difference was
entirely who it sounded like.

## 04 — The insight

The register is not a decoration on top of B2B content. It is the thing
deciding whether the content gets read at all, and it can be changed
without lowering the standard of what is being said.

That is a more useful conclusion than "be more casual", because it gives
you a test: does this sound like the category, or does it sound like
somebody who has actually been in the reader's situation?

## 05 — The strategy

- **Different registers for different audiences under one brand.**
  Cybersecurity content stayed precise and technical. Careers and
  upskilling content was allowed to sound like a person. Both were
  recognisably from the same company.
- **Formats matched to attention, not to preference.** Infographics for
  things that are structurally visual, newsletters for things that need
  a running relationship, SEO articles for things people actively search.
- **Campaign moments as anchors.** Women's Day, Black History Month and
  Mental Health Month gave the calendar fixed points with a genuine
  reason to exist, rather than posting into a void on a schedule.
- **Career Rizz 101** — a series aimed at a younger professional
  audience, in a voice that matched them. Meme marketing and polls were
  part of the mix here, used where the audience actually was.

## 06 — The work

- Long-form SEO articles across AI, cybersecurity, upskilling and digital
  transformation
- Email campaigns and recurring newsletters
- Infographics translating dense topics into something scannable
- Social content, including polls and meme formats where the audience
  and platform justified it
- **Career Rizz 101** — a careers-focused campaign series
- Campaign execution for Women's Day, Black History Month and Mental
  Health Month
- Ongoing funnel optimisation informed by Google Analytics and
  performance data

## 07 — The result

**Weekly active users grew 400%** over the period.

The honest framing: that is a team number over eight months, not a single
campaign's number, and I was one contributor to it. What I can attribute
more directly is which content earned repetition — the analytics work was
specifically about deciding what to do more of, and the mix shifted
noticeably as a result.

## 08 — What I learned

That "professional" and "readable" were never actually in tension — the
category just behaves as though they are, and nobody checks.

Also that funnel optimisation with real analytics access changes how you
write. Once you can see where people stop, you stop defending the
paragraph you liked and start fixing the one they left on.
