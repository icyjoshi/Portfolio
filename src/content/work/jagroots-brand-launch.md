## 01 — The brief

Take a location-based social networking app from "we are nearly ready" to
"people have heard of us". The launch date was fixed. The audience was
not defined in any usable way, and the category — apps that connect you
to people physically near you — does not have the kind of shorthand that
lets a one-line ad do the work for you.

## 02 — The context

A new consumer app has a specific and unforgiving problem. It is not that
people dislike it. It is that an empty product is genuinely less useful
than a full one, so the first users get the worst version of the
experience and the marketing has to be good enough to carry them through
it anyway.

So the research had to cover more than messaging. I worked through the
market, the competitive set, and the pricing, distribution and packaging
questions — because for a product like this, how you package and
distribute it *is* part of the positioning, not a downstream decision
somebody makes later.

## 03 — The observation

The competitive content in this space almost all does the same thing: it
explains the concept. Diagrams of people connecting. Copy about
community. A lot of describing.

Which is a reasonable instinct for an unfamiliar category, and also the
reason so much of it slides past without landing. Explaining a social
product is a poor substitute for showing one being used, because the
appeal of the thing is not the mechanism — it is the specific small
moment the mechanism produces.

## 04 — The insight

If the category has no shorthand, you do not build the shorthand by
defining it. You build it by showing the same recognisable moment often
enough that people supply the definition themselves.

That reframed the launch from an explanation problem into a casting and
scripting problem. The question stopped being "how do we describe what
this does" and became "what is the moment somebody would screenshot and
send to a friend".

## 05 — The strategy

Three decisions came out of that:

1. **Lead with situations, not features.** Every piece of launch content
   opens on a scenario a viewer recognises, and lets the product appear
   as the thing that resolves it.
2. **Write for the first three seconds.** Reel scripts built around a
   hook that works without sound and without prior knowledge of the app.
3. **Do not carry the whole launch on paid or organic social alone.**
   Offline outreach ran in parallel, because a location-based product has
   the unusual advantage of being able to recruit users in the actual
   locations it serves.

The research fed all three. Competitive analysis told me which openings
were already worn out. The pricing, distribution and packaging work
decided what we could credibly promise at launch.

## 06 — The work

- **Go-to-market research** — market sizing and category read,
  competitive analysis, and the pricing, distribution and packaging study
  that shaped what the launch could actually claim.
- **Content direction for the launch** — the scenario-led format, the
  rules for what each piece had to do, and the brief the creative team
  worked from.
- **Reel scripts** — written to the three-second rule, sound-off first.
- **Offline outreach** — on-ground activity aligned to the same
  positioning as the digital content.
- **Multi-client content planning** — calendars and direction across the
  agency's other accounts, running alongside the launch.
- **Website structure and content architecture** — a rebuild of how the
  site was organised, with SEO and GEO work on top of it.

## 07 — The result

The launch content reached **2× the views** of the brand's previous
content.

The website relaunch, run in parallel, produced **3× the traffic** it had
before — that one is more attributable, because restructuring
information architecture and fixing search visibility has a fairly direct
line to sessions.

The launch number I would treat more carefully. A launch has novelty
working in its favour and one campaign is not a pattern. It was the right
direction; it was not yet proof of a repeatable system.

## 08 — What I learned

Two things.

First, that packaging and distribution research is not the boring
pre-work before the creative starts. It sets the boundary of what the
creative is allowed to say, and doing it first made the content decisions
faster rather than slower.

Second, that "explain the category" is the default instinct for anything
unfamiliar, and it is usually wrong. Recognition beats explanation almost
every time, and it is harder to write, which is probably why the
explanation version is so common.
